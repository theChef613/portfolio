No, MBSE Isn't the Future—It's Always Been Here

Every few years, the engineering industry adopts a new catchphrase and elevates it to silver-bullet status. Right now, Model-Based Systems Engineering (MBSE) is receiving the full hype-cycle treatment—frequently pitched as a revolutionary, magic solution to all complex engineering problems, much like Artificial Intelligence is marketed today.

But here is the reality: MBSE isn't a new discipline, nor is it some distant future state. It is simply systems engineering performed using the modern tools available to us today.

More importantly, MBSE doesn't exist in a vacuum. It is just one component of a much larger, industry-wide shift toward Digital Engineering—transforming not just how we design systems, but how hardware, software, testing, and lifecycle management interconnect across the entire enterprise.

From Slide Rules to Spreadsheets

To understand where we are going, it helps to look back at how engineering tools have evolved over time.

When engineers transitioned from slide rules and draft paper to digital tools like Microsoft Excel, the fundamental principles of math, physics, and financial analysis didn't change. What changed was the toolset, the speed of execution, and the ability to update data dynamically without starting from scratch.

Watching early demos of software like Excel highlights just how transformative basic features like AutoSum and AutoFill were at the time. Excel marked a major industry shift where repetitive, manual mechanics were offloaded to software.

Today, we are standing in that exact same transition zone.

A 1990s Excel Commercial, heralding a shift from slide rules and paper to software driven analysis

From Fragmented Artifacts to Linked Models

For years, engineering disciplines operated in silos, relying on localized spreadsheets, isolated CAD files, custom calculation scripts, and static PDF reports. Data management between sub-teams often meant fishing through scattered email threads, instant messages, or phone calls to verify if a requirement or mass property had changed.

MBSE provides the structural backbone, but it powers a broader Digital Engineering Landscape:

  • Authoritative Source of Truth (ASOT): Instead of twenty conflicting versions of a spreadsheet stored on local hard drives, system parameters live in a single, authoritative architecture model that feeds all engineering domains.
  • Linked Models & Agile Implementation: Requirements, behavior, detailed design, and physical architecture are connected downstream. When a constraint or requirement shifts, connected models across disciplines update dynamically, enabling rapid iteration and agile engineering cycles.
  • Agentic Documentation: Paperwork and compliance artifacts are no longer compiled manually by copying and pasting data across static documents. Instead, intelligent workflows auto-generate documentation straight from the live digital ecosystem.
  • System-Contextual Chatbots: Embedded AI assistants can query the connected system model directly, giving engineers immediate visibility into requirements traceability, interface definitions, or design trade-offs without needing to manually sift through hundreds of pages of specs.

Where Do Engineers Fit in This New Landscape?

When models across disciplines are hyper-connected and documentation generation becomes agentic, it is natural to ask: What is left for the systems engineer to do?

The answer is simple: We determine what the system needs to accomplish, and we determine what is physically possible.

Tools can manage data relationships, generate reports, and propagate parameter changes instantly across thousands of requirements. But tools cannot evaluate multi-domain trade-offs, challenge invalid assumptions, or synthesize intent.

The primary job of the engineer shifts away from data wrangling and administrative upkeep toward core engineering:

  1. Defining Requirements & Scope: Understanding stakeholder needs, defining system boundary conditions, and articulating what the system must achieve.
  2. Evaluating Feasibility: Applying domain knowledge and physics-based judgment to assess whether a proposed solution is actually viable.

MBSE and Digital Engineering aren't about replacing engineering judgment with software models; they are about freeing engineers from manual bookkeeping and analysis cycles so they can spend their time engineering.
